Use this framework as a diagnostic reasoning tool for analyzing persistent governance failure or underperformance in a country, institution, or sector such as health, energy, housing, water, or transport.

Do not treat the framework as a proven causal model or a checklist in which every factor must be present. Use evidence to determine which factors are relevant, how they interact, and which are symptoms versus root causes.

Distinguish clearly between:

- **Documented facts**
- **Reported or alleged claims**
- **Reasonable inferences**
- **Uncertain conclusions**

Do not assume that correlation proves causation. Test competing explanations, chronology, counterfactuals, and alternative interpretations.

**Core diagnostic objectives**

Determine:

1.       What outcome is failing?

2.       Which GAF factors are bottlenecks?

3.       Which bottlenecks are causally connected?

4.       Whether the problem is transient, institutional, structural, or externally sustained.

5.       Whether reform requires Stage 0 elite consensus.

6.       Which reforms can realistically change incentives.

7.       Which indicators would confirm or falsify the diagnosis.

---

**Part A: Governance Assessment Factors**

For each factor, classify the current state as:

- **Healthy**
- **Bottleneck**
- **Uncertain**
- **Not applicable**

Support each classification with specific evidence.

**GAF-1: Accountability Diffusion**

Can citizens, voters, users, or affected groups identify and sanction the actor responsible for failure?

- **Healthy:** One clearly identifiable actor or authority can be held responsible.
- **Bottleneck:** Responsibility is divided among many actors; blame circulates without corrective action.
- **Diagnostic:** Count independent actors with veto or approval power and identify who can impose consequences.

**GAF-2: Stakeholder Heterogeneity**

How many independent decision-makers must approve or coordinate for the system to function?

- **Healthy:** Few actors with aligned incentives and a unified mandate.
- **Bottleneck:** Numerous actors, agencies, regions, municipalities, providers, or political blocs possess veto power.
- **Diagnostic:** Map approval chains, veto points, conflicting mandates, and fragmented authority.

**GAF-3: Time-Horizon Mismatch**

Does the infrastructure or reform payback period exceed the political and electoral cycle?

- **Healthy:** Long-term projects are protected across political cycles.
- **Bottleneck:** Projects requiring many years are governed through short electoral cycles and annual renegotiation.
- **Diagnostic:** Compare project payback period, election cycle, budget cycle, leadership turnover, and legal protection.

**GAF-4: Stakeholder Capture Mechanism**

Do powerful actors profit from scarcity, inefficiency, delay, or restricted capacity growth?

- **Healthy:** Revenue comes primarily from delivering value and expanding capacity.
- **Bottleneck:** Actors benefit financially from scarcity, bottlenecks, exclusion, or inefficient structures.
- **Diagnostic:** Identify rents, concentrated revenue, incumbent protection, artificial scarcity, and actors who would lose from reform.

**GAF-5: Procedural Drag**

How long do regulatory, environmental, planning, permitting, and land-acquisition processes take compared with relevant international or technical baselines?

- **Healthy:** Processes are time-limited, parallelized where possible, adequately staffed, and enforceable.
- **Bottleneck:** Reviews are prolonged, sequential, indefinite, or routinely exceed reasonable technical requirements.
- **Diagnostic:** Separate genuinely necessary technical work from administrative delay, staffing shortages, sequencing problems, and absent deadlines.

**GAF-6: Transparent KPIs and Accountability**

Are performance targets public, measurable, binding, and consequential?

- **Healthy:** Metrics are published regularly; missed targets trigger documented consequences.
- **Bottleneck:** Metrics are hidden, selectively defined, nonbinding, changed after failure, or disconnected from sanctions.
- **Diagnostic:** Examine whether the public can identify failure and whether responsible actors face consequences.

**GAF-7: Information Asymmetry**

Can citizens and affected users evaluate outcomes, or must they rely entirely on official or expert claims?

- **Healthy:** Independent, outcome-based metrics are available.
- **Bottleneck:** Operators publish favorable throughput or uptime metrics while concealing quality, access, congestion, mortality, readmissions, queues, or distributional effects.
- **Diagnostic:** Compare reported activity with actual outcomes and user experience.

**GAF-8: Equity Penalty**

What share of revenue or capacity must be used to serve unprofitable populations, regions, or services?

- **Healthy:** Cross-subsidies are explicit, protected, and sufficient to maintain comparable service.
- **Bottleneck:** Loss-making groups are systematically underserved, abandoned, or separated into inferior tiers.
- **Diagnostic:** Compare service quality, waiting times, investment, staffing, and access across profitable/unprofitable and urban/rural segments.

A cross-subsidy requirement above approximately 15% should be examined as a potential structural pressure point, not treated as an automatic failure.

**GAF-9: Institutional Capacity to Execute**

Can the responsible government or institution recruit, train, retain, manage, and deploy personnel to implement policy?

- **Healthy:** Staffing is sufficient and stable; promotions are merit-based; implementation follows policy design.
- **Bottleneck:** Policies are repeatedly announced but not implemented because the institution lacks staff, skills, management, or retention.
- **Diagnostic:** Compare announced policies with actual delivery, vacancies, staffing stability, implementation dates, and retention.

**GAF-10: Workforce Shortage**

Is there a structural shortage of trained professionals rather than merely a wage problem?

- **Healthy:** Vacancies, absenteeism, migration, and workload are manageable.
- **Bottleneck:** Persistent vacancies, burnout, emigration, or absenteeism constrain capacity despite wage increases.
- **Diagnostic:** Examine vacancies, training pipelines, migration, workload, burnout, regional distribution, and whether higher pay changes retention.

**GAF-11: Financial Sustainability Model**

Is revenue protected from political cycles, or must it be renegotiated repeatedly?

- **Healthy:** Multi-year funding uses automatic adjustment for wages, inflation, demographics, or demand.
- **Bottleneck:** Annual or coalition-based renegotiation creates recurring deficits, uncertainty, and underinvestment.
- **Diagnostic:** Check budget formulas, execution guarantees, adjustment mechanisms, deficit history, and political veto points.

**GAF-12: Capacity-Constraint Type**

What fundamentally limits the sector?

Classify the constraint as one or more of:

- **Physics-limited:** Capacity requires long-term infrastructure, as in electricity grids.
- **Demand-elastic:** Capacity can adjust relatively quickly to demand, as in some services.
- **Capital-scarce:** Investment finance is the binding constraint.
- **Workforce-constrained:** Skilled personnel are the binding constraint.
- **Geography-constrained:** Distance, terrain, regional distribution, or territorial reach is decisive.
- **Healthy:** Policy explicitly identifies the relevant constraint.
- **Bottleneck:** The sector is governed according to the wrong constraint model.
- **Diagnostic:** Determine whether the chosen governance model matches the sector’s physical, financial, labor, and geographic characteristics.

**GAF-13: Profitability Distribution**

Do profitable segments subsidize unprofitable segments, or do providers cherry-pick profitable activity?

- **Healthy:** High-margin segments fund low-margin services, regions, populations, or time periods.
- **Bottleneck:** Investment and service quality concentrate in profitable areas while loss-making areas deteriorate.
- **Diagnostic:** Compare investment, staffing, service availability, and provider incentives across segments.

**GAF-14: Participation Capture Risk**

Can organized minorities, local coalitions, or NIMBY groups veto projects that benefit the broader population?

- **Healthy:** Participation improves design and legitimacy without creating indefinite veto power.
- **Bottleneck:** Local or organized groups can block system-wide projects without an effective override or compensation mechanism.
- **Diagnostic:** Measure project cancellations, delays, local vetoes, appeal cycles, and whether participation is consultative or determinative.

**GAF-15: Crisis Visibility and Urgency**

Is system failure visible to voters, businesses, media, and affected users?

Assess:

- How many people experience failure directly?
- Whether media coverage is increasing.
- Whether the trend is worsening.
- Whether the failure is framed as a domestic governance problem or as an external inevitability.
- **Healthy pressure condition:** High visibility, worsening outcomes, and broad recognition of domestic responsibility.
- **Bottleneck condition:** Failure is hidden by optimistic metrics, remittances, external funding, or externalized explanations.

High visibility plus a worsening trajectory can create a window for Stage 0 consensus. Low visibility may allow silent institutional hollowing.

**GAF-16: Institutional Learning Capacity**

Does the system analyze failed reforms and adapt, or repeat the same intervention?

- **Healthy:** Failed reforms are evaluated publicly; subsequent reforms explicitly incorporate lessons.
- **Bottleneck:** The same targets and solutions are repeatedly announced without root-cause analysis.
- **Diagnostic:** Look for post-mortems, evaluation quality, documented learning, and changes in policy design rather than merely new funding or implementation plans.

**GAF-17: Revenue Stability and Automatic Adjustment**

Does revenue for capacity investment grow automatically with wages, inflation, demographics, or demand?

- **Healthy:** Automatic formulas protect long-term capacity investment.
- **Bottleneck:** Revenue depends on annual political negotiation.
- **Diagnostic:** Examine whether funding adjusts automatically and whether political actors can easily reduce it.

GAF-11 concerns the broader financial sustainability model; GAF-17 focuses specifically on automatic revenue protection.

**GAF-18: Democratic Deficit–Execution Capacity Trade-off**

Is the balance between democratic accountability and long-term execution appropriate for the sector?

- **Physics-limited sectors:** May require long-term autonomy, protected budgets, and safeguards against short-cycle politics.
- **Demand-elastic sectors:** May tolerate shorter political cycles and more direct democratic adjustment.
- **Healthy:** The level of autonomy, veto power, oversight, and reversibility matches the sector’s constraint type.
- **Bottleneck:** Either excessive veto points create gridlock or excessive autonomy creates unaccountable power.

Pure majoritarian control can produce short-termism and gridlock. Pure bureaucratic autonomy can produce extraction and abuse. Assess the trade-off rather than assuming that more democracy or more autonomy is always better.

**GAF-19: Cross-Border and Cross-Regional Coordination**

Can the relevant system coordinate across regions, municipalities, countries, grids, markets, or supply chains?

- **Healthy:** Authority is unified or coordination mechanisms align incentives.
- **Bottleneck:** Fragmented authority and external dependencies prevent isolated reform.
- **Diagnostic:** Identify interconnection, trade, regulatory, infrastructure, and jurisdictional dependencies.

**GAF-20: Path Dependency and Reversibility**

Once implemented, how easily can a reform be reversed?

- **Healthy:** Effective reforms are protected long enough to be evaluated; reversal requires appropriate scrutiny.
- **Bottleneck:** Simple majorities can repeatedly reverse policy, or ideology makes rational revision politically taboo.
- **Diagnostic:** Examine supermajority requirements, constitutional protections, sunk costs, legal continuity, and the political legitimacy of reversal.

Distinguish:

- **Technical irreversibility:** Reversal is legally or materially difficult.
- **Pseudo-irreversibility:** Reversal is technically possible but politically treated as betrayal or illegitimate.

**GAF-21: Rule of Law and Institutional Independence**

Can courts, regulators, oversight bodies, electoral authorities, prosecutors, and auditors operate independently of executives, ruling parties, economic elites, or security institutions?

- **Healthy:** Appointment, removal, budgetary, and operational rules protect independence; decisions are reasoned and consistently enforced.
- **Bottleneck:** Institutions apply rules selectively, lack autonomy, or become extensions of political or economic networks.
- **Diagnostic:** Examine appointments, investigations, sanctions, budgets, enforcement consistency, and whether oversight findings produce consequences.

Strong execution without independent oversight can produce durable but unaccountable governance.

**GAF-22: Territorial State Reach and Local Implementation**

Can national policy be delivered consistently across regions, municipalities, rural areas, urban areas, and vulnerable communities?

- **Healthy:** Local institutions possess staffing, funding, data, authority, and accountability while meeting national standards.
- **Bottleneck:** Policy works in the capital or major cities but fails elsewhere.
- **Diagnostic:** Compare regional staffing, infrastructure, service quality, enforcement, project completion, and geographic access.

National averages can conceal severe local failure.

**GAF-23: Social Legitimacy and Civic Trust**

Do citizens and affected groups regard institutions and reforms as legitimate enough to comply with, participate in, and sustain them?

- **Healthy:** Institutions explain decisions, accept scrutiny, incorporate credible participation, and deliver reasonably consistent results.
- **Bottleneck:** Reforms are seen as imposed, partisan, captured, coercive, or arbitrary.
- **Diagnostic:** Examine trust in government, courts, parties, media, experts, statistics, and the perceived fairness of participation and enforcement.

Administrative capacity cannot sustain reforms that citizens regard as illegitimate.

**GAF-24: Ideological Lock-in and Alternative Foreclosure**

Do dominant ideologies prevent decision-makers from considering alternatives to the existing institutional model?

This differs from:

- **GAF-16:** Failure to learn from implementation.
- **GAF-20:** Difficulty reversing existing institutions.
- **GAF-24:** Refusal to question the underlying model or framing.
- **Healthy:** Alternatives are explicitly considered and compared using evidence and cost-benefit analysis.
- **Bottleneck:** Repeated failure is attributed only to poor implementation, staffing, or coordination; alternative models are dismissed as impossible, immoral, or incompatible with national identity.
- **Diagnostic signals:**

- The same policy goals are repeatedly announced without root-cause analysis.
- Debate is restricted to improving the existing model.
- Structural alternatives are rejected without comparison.
- Core design choices are treated as morally or ideologically non-negotiable.
- Reversal is framed as betrayal rather than evidence-based adaptation.

Ideological lock-in can make equity problems invisible, block institutional learning, sanctify veto points, prevent coordination, and create pseudo-irreversibility.

**GAF-25: Recognition, Identity, and Status Grievance**

Do significant groups believe that their identity, history, language, religion, territory, dignity, or status is being erased, subordinated, or deliberately disrespected?

- **Healthy:** Identities and historical grievances can be recognized through lawful channels without granting permanent veto power.
- **Bottleneck:** Political competition is framed as existential group survival; the state is perceived to belong to another group.
- **Diagnostic:** Examine identity-based messaging, unequal representation, historical humiliation, exclusion from state symbols, and perceived demographic or cultural displacement.

Unaddressed recognition grievances can progress from perceived loss to mobilization, polarization, institutional distrust, and extra-institutional conflict.

**GAF-26: Power Concentration and Succession Risk**

How dependent is governance on one leader, family, party, faction, military institution, or patronage network? Can power transfer peacefully?

- **Healthy:** Leaders are replaceable; term limits and succession rules are credible; former leaders can leave safely.
- **Bottleneck:** The leader controls courts, elections, security, media, or major economic networks; opponents are existential enemies; departure threatens imprisonment, confiscation, or death.
- **Diagnostic:** Assess credible successors, elite exit options, security neutrality, term limits, electoral acceptance, and personalist control.

Power concentration plus unsafe exit creates a no-exit problem and raises the risk of repression, external conflict, or succession crisis.

**GAF-27: Coercive Power and Security-Sector Autonomy**

Who controls organized coercion, and is there one lawful, accountable chain of command?

- **Healthy:** Military, police, intelligence, paramilitary, and armed actors are professionally managed, politically neutral, auditable, and subordinate to civilian law.
- **Bottleneck:** Multiple armed chains of command control territory, revenue, businesses, or political outcomes.
- **Diagnostic:** Map armed actors, command structures, revenue sources, territorial control, factional recruitment, budgets, and demobilization capacity.

Security fragmentation is more dangerous than administrative fragmentation because veto power is backed by force.

**GAF-28: Polarization and Cross-Cutting Cleavages**

Do political divisions cross-cut one another, or do identity, geography, class, religion, language, ideology, and economic divisions reinforce each other?

- **Healthy:** Parties and institutions contain diverse groups; opponents share a common institutional identity; coalitions cross social cleavages.
- **Bottleneck:** Parties represent mutually exclusive communities; opponents are treated as traitors; electoral defeat threatens group survival.
- **Diagnostic:** Assess cleavage overlap, media separation, political compromise, acceptance of defeat, and whether divisions are becoming mutually reinforcing.

Polarization risk increases with division intensity, cleavage overlap, and institutional weakness.

**GAF-29: Information-Ecosystem Integrity and Manipulability**

Can citizens and institutions form political judgments from plural, independent, and verifiable information?

- **Healthy:** Multiple independent sources exist; political funding is traceable; errors are corrected transparently; journalists and researchers are protected.
- **Bottleneck:** State or private actors control distribution, coordinate disinformation, dehumanize opponents, attack fact-checking, or flood the public with contradictory claims.
- **Diagnostic:** Examine media ownership, digital distribution, coordinated messaging, foreign influence, political advertising, censorship, and attacks on independent verification.

GAF-29 concerns deliberate political manipulation. It differs from GAF-7, which concerns operational or citizen–provider information asymmetry.

**GAF-30: Peaceful Contestation and Redress Capacity**

Can citizens and groups challenge decisions, seek remedies, and transfer conflict into lawful institutions?

- **Healthy:** Courts, ombuds offices, legislatures, elections, protest, mediation, and collective bargaining provide credible remedies within predictable timeframes.
- **Bottleneck:** Complaints accumulate without remedy; courts are inaccessible or captured; elections cannot change power; protest is the only effective route; dissent is repressed.
- **Diagnostic:** Examine access to justice, electoral competitiveness, lawful protest, complaint resolution, minority rights, and whether losers accept decisions.

Without credible redress, grievances move from institutional participation toward noncompliance, repression, or violence.

**GAF-31: External Dependency and Conflict-Sustaining Resource Flows**

Do external actors, markets, remittances, aid, military assistance, sanctions, illicit finance, or strategic resources sustain dysfunctional governance?

- **Healthy:** External funding is transparent, aligned with public institutions, diversified, and subject to domestic oversight.
- **Bottleneck:** Leaders or armed groups survive without domestic consent because of foreign finance, military support, aid, resource rents, remittances, or external sponsors.
- **Diagnostic:** Map external revenue, sponsors, aid channels, resource ownership, sanctions, remittance dependence, parallel structures, and foreign veto power.

GAF-19 concerns coordination across borders or regions. GAF-31 concerns whether external relationships sustain the bottleneck itself.

**GAF-32: Shock Absorption and Adaptive Resilience**

Can the system absorb economic, demographic, environmental, technological, health, security, or leadership shocks without losing basic legitimacy and functionality?

- **Healthy:** Emergency powers are temporary, reviewable, proportionate, and do not permanently weaken oversight; essential services continue; vulnerable groups are protected; institutions learn.
- **Bottleneck:** Every shock produces breakdown, concealment, permanent emergency powers, elite protection, unequal territorial effects, or permanent power concentration.
- **Diagnostic:** Assess contingency planning, fiscal buffers, emergency procurement, crisis learning, distributional effects, continuity of services, and post-crisis institutional recovery.

GAF-16 measures learning from failure. GAF-32 measures whether the entire system withstands stress without converting a temporary shock into a structural political crisis.

**GAF-33: Administrative Overhang and Mandate–Capacity Mismatch**

**Question:** Does the administrative structure impose more organizational, procedural, territorial, and fiscal obligations than the state can competently manage?

**Healthy:** Mandates are prioritized, agencies have clear responsibilities, staffing matches workload, procedures are proportionate, and administrative costs are sustainable.

**Bottleneck:** The state maintains overlapping agencies, excessive procedures, unfunded mandates, vacant technical positions, large fixed costs, or reporting requirements that absorb capacity without improving outcomes.

**Uncertain:** The structure appears large or complex, but evidence is insufficient to establish that its size or complexity causes poor performance.

**Diagnostic:** Compare the following ratios and relationships:

- Number of ministries and agencies with population and revenue
- Formal mandates with actual staffing
- Approved positions with filled positions
- Administrative spending with frontline service spending
- Number of approval stages and processing times
- Overlapping or conflicting legal mandates
- Senior-management layers
- Payroll costs and pension obligations
- Central-government responsibilities versus territorial reach
- Policy announcements versus actual implementation

**Part B: Post-Colonial Institutional Legacy**

Use PCIL factors only where a relevant formal colonial or post-colonial institutional history exists. Do not force PCIL explanations onto cases where they do not apply.

A bottleneck may be:

- **Structural:** rooted in inherited institutional path dependency and likely requiring Stage 0 elite consensus.
- **Transient:** produced primarily by current policy, budgeting, staffing, or implementation choices and potentially solvable through ordinary governance.

**FOUNDATIONAL FACTORS (PCIL-1 to PCIL-8)**

**PCIL-1: Institutional Orphaning**

After the departure of a mentor, imperial, or external governing authority, long-term accountability and commitment mechanisms disappear.

Common effects:

- GAF-1: Accountability diffusion
- GAF-3: Time-horizon mismatch
- GAF-11: Financial instability
- GAF-17: Absence of automatic revenue protection

Usually requires Stage 0 consensus.

**PCIL-2: Patronage Institutionalization**

Patronage becomes the default method of allocating jobs, contracts, approvals, protection, and resources.

Common effects:

- GAF-2: Stakeholder heterogeneity
- GAF-4: Stakeholder capture
- GAF-26: Power concentration
- GAF-29: Information manipulation

Usually requires Stage 0 consensus.

**PCIL-3: Differentiated Corruption Origins**

Distinguish:

- **Regulatory-margin corruption:** localized, auditable, and potentially manageable through transparency and enforcement.
- **Systemic corruption:** embedded in elite bargains and institutional structures.

Common effects:

- GAF-6: Weak or selective KPIs
- GAF-7: Information asymmetry
- GAF-21: Weak institutional independence

Regulatory-margin corruption may be solvable without Stage 0. Systemic corruption generally requires it.

**PCIL-4: Brain Drain as Capacity Sink**

Educated and trained workers leave because of poor governance, weak institutions, insecurity, low professional autonomy, or burnout. Remittances can conceal the resulting institutional hollowing.

Common effects:

- GAF-9: Institutional incapacity
- GAF-10: Workforce shortage
- GAF-15: Low crisis visibility
- GAF-32: Weak resilience

Usually requires Stage 0 consensus because wage increases alone may not address the causes of emigration.

**PCIL-5: Regulatory Complexity Without Execution Capacity**

Inherited or accumulated regulatory codes exceed the state’s staffing, technical, and administrative capacity.

Common effects:

- GAF-5: Procedural drag
- GAF-9: Institutional incapacity

Often solvable without Stage 0 through simplification, parallel processing, staffing, and enforceable deadlines.

**PCIL-6: Ethnic or Geographic Fiefdom Mapping**

Patronage and resource allocation follow inherited ethnic, regional, coastal/interior, or metropolitan divisions.

Common effects:

- GAF-2: Stakeholder heterogeneity
- GAF-8: Equity penalty
- GAF-13: Profitability asymmetry
- GAF-22: Unequal territorial reach

Usually requires Stage 0 consensus.

**PCIL-7: Traumatic Institutional Rupture**

Coups, civil war, state collapse, or violent institutional rupture destroy trust in courts, bureaucracies, experts, parties, and democratic procedures.

Common effects:

- GAF-1: Accountability breakdown
- GAF-7: Information asymmetry and distrust
- GAF-14: Participation capture
- GAF-23: Low legitimacy
- GAF-30: Weak redress

Usually requires long-term Stage 0 consensus and institutional rebuilding.

**PCIL-8: Demographic Regionalism and Governance Mismatch**

Regions function as politically autonomous actors, often disguising patronage or identity-based competition as administrative autonomy.

Common effects:

- GAF-2: Stakeholder heterogeneity
- GAF-19: Coordination failure
- GAF-22: Territorial implementation gaps
- GAF-28: Reinforced polarization

Usually requires Stage 0 consensus about the appropriate distribution of authority.

**INVISIBLE PERSISTENCE MECHANISMS (PCIL-9 to PCIL-15)**

  
**PCIL-9: Institutional Amnesia**

After decolonization or after traumatic rupture,

dominant groups lose institutional memory of

why certain structures, categories, or safeguards exist.

Minority groups retain the memory and grievance.

Common effects:

- - GAF-23: Legitimacy collapse (minorities see dismissal)
- - GAF-7: Information asymmetry (majority unaware of exclusion)
- - GAF-29: Asymmetric information standards (minority claims require proof; majority claims accepted)

Usually requires Stage 0 consensus.  
  
  
  
**PCIL-10: Presumption Reversal**

Majority claims are accepted with minimal verification; minority claims require exhaustive proof. The asymmetry appears procedurally neutral but is substantively skewed.

Common effects:

- GAF-7: Information asymmetry (burden of proof unequally distributed)
- GAF-29: Asymmetric information standards (minority evidence rejected; majority evidence accepted)
- GAF-23: Legitimacy collapse (minorities perceive institutional bad faith)

Usually requires Stage 0 consensus.

**PCIL-11: Category Naturalization**

Inherited administrative, ethnic, or geographic categories appear neutral/technical. Reclassification is treated as "administrative burden," not political necessity. Categories become invisible as categories—they appear natural rather than constructed.

Common effects:

- GAF-22: Territorial inequality (service gaps align with inherited category boundaries)
- GAF-8: Equity penalty (cross-subsidies skipped for "hard-to-serve" categories)
- GAF-24: Ideological capture (category structure reframed as natural/unavoidable)

Definition: Inherited administrative, ethnic, or class

categories appear neutral/technical but perpetuate historical

exclusion. Reclassification is resisted as "administrative burden."

Compounds: PCIL-1 (orphaning created the categories),

PCIL-6 (fiefdom mapping depends on category stability)

Detection: Look for service data that clusters by inherited admin boundaries, not current need.

Usually requires Stage 0 consensus (categories defend themselves institutionally)

**PCIL-12: Strategic Denial**

When past institutional harm is documented, institutions respond with procedural correctness rather than accountability. Defense becomes indistinguishable from indifference, making denial appear legitimate.

Common effects:

- GAF-23: Legitimacy collapse (victims see procedural responses as evasion)
- GAF-1: Accountability diffusion (responsibility absorbed into procedure)
- GAF-25: Grievance exclusion (historical claims processed but not acted upon)

Usually requires Stage 0 consensus.

---

**PCIL-13: Intergenerational Transmission**

Majority learns institutions exist "for them"; minorities learn institutions are adversarial. Differential institutional approach appears as "cultural attitude" or inherited behavior rather than institutional behavior producing attitudes.

Common effects:

- GAF-2: Stakeholder heterogeneity (groups have opposite institutional relationships)
- GAF-23: Legitimacy collapse (divergent trust levels appear inherent, not institutional)
- GAF-24: Ideological capture (institutional exclusion reframed as cultural preference)

Usually requires Stage 0 consensus.

---

**PCIL-14: Algorithmic Embedding**

Historical hierarchies are reproduced at scale by code. Bias appears technical, not political. Correction attempts fix outcomes without addressing origins, causing bias to reappear through new algorithmic pathways.

Common effects:

- GAF-22: Territorial inequality (algorithmic service allocation reproduces geographic hierarchy)
- GAF-6: Hidden metrics (bias embedded in parameters treated as objective design)
- GAF-7: Information asymmetry (bias recognized as technical problem, not institutional one)

Usually requires Stage 0 consensus.

---

**PCIL-15: Documentary Asymmetry**

One side has institutional records; the other has official silence reframed as "no evidence." Interpretation authority and narrative control remain unequally distributed.

Common effects:

- GAF-9: Institutional forgetting (documented claims treated as anecdotal; undocumented institutional practices treated as baseline)
- GAF-23: Legitimacy collapse (minorities perceive documentary record as controlled)
- GAF-25: Grievance exclusion (claims unsupported by institutional archives are dismissed)

Usually requires Stage 0 consensus.

**Part C: Core Interdependencies**

Do not recommend fixing one factor in isolation when its linked factor would recreate the same bottleneck.

**Administrative and sectoral relationships**

- **GAF-1 ↔ GAF-2:** Fragmented decision-making diffuses accountability. Assigning responsibility without reducing veto points leaves the responsible actor unable to act.
- **GAF-4 ↔ GAF-6/GAF-7:** Capture survives when rents are invisible. Transparency without consequences produces information without reform.
- **GAF-3 ↔ GAF-11/GAF-17:** Long-term projects fail when short political cycles are combined with annual funding uncertainty.
- **GAF-5 ↔ GAF-1/GAF-6:** Streamlining procedures without accountable deadlines allows actors to slow-walk projects.
- **GAF-10 ↔ GAF-11:** Workforce shortages require sustained funding; one-time wage increases or annual budgets may worsen emigration and burnout.
- **GAF-8 ↔ GAF-13/GAF-11:** Equity requires cross-subsidy and protected revenue. Otherwise profitable segments are preserved while loss-making segments deteriorate.
- **GAF-12 ↔ GAF-18:** Governance must match the constraint type. Physics-limited sectors need long-term autonomy; demand-elastic sectors can tolerate shorter cycles.

**New Interdependencies Involving GAF-33**

**GAF-33 ↔ GAF-5:** Administrative overhang creates fragmented mandates and resource dilution that produce procedural drag. However, procedural drag can occur in a well-sized administration. Distinguish: (a) **Procedural Drag** = friction within processes; (b) **Administrative Overhang** = mismatch between the entire system and available capacity. Both can coexist. Reducing procedures without eliminating overlap may achieve temporary speed gains while structural overhang persists.

**GAF-33 ↔ GAF-9:** Institutional capacity to execute depends partly on staffing but also on whether the institution's mandates exceed what any staffing level can deliver. GAF-9 asks "Can the institution implement?" GAF-33 asks "Is the institution's mandate realistic given available resources?" A well-staffed ministry performing an oversized mandate remains a bottleneck.

**GAF-33 ↔ GAF-10:** Workforce shortage and administrative overhang interact. Vacancies become more severe when the system requires specialized expertise spread across many overlapping agencies. Wage increases alone may not address the underlying mandate-capacity mismatch.

**GAF-33 ↔ GAF-11 & GAF-17:** Financial sustainability depends on whether revenue can support mandates. If administrative costs (salaries, management layers, unfunded obligations) consume revenue that should fund frontline services, both GAF-11 and GAF-33 are bottlenecks. Neither revenue protection nor wage growth resolves structural overhang.

**GAF-33 ↔ GAF-21:** Rule of law and institutional independence require oversight bodies to operate independently. Administrative overhang weakens independence when patronage networks become necessary to allocate scarce capacity across overlapping mandates.

**GAF-33 ↔ GAF-22:** Territorial state reach and local implementation. A central government with mandates exceeding its reach struggles to deliver consistently. Overhang is more severe when responsibilities extend to regions the state cannot competently govern.

**Part D: Causal Chains**

Use these chains as hypotheses, not automatic conclusions.

**Identity escalation**

Unequal outcomes → identity grievance → polarization → manipulated threat narratives → trust collapse → failure of peaceful contestation → conflict risk.

Breaking inequality without recognition may leave the core grievance intact. Recognition without redress may produce acknowledgment without remedy.

**Authoritarian succession crisis**

Power concentration → institutional capture → security fragmentation → absent accountability → hidden crisis → inability to absorb leadership shock → coup, civil war, or state collapse.

External support may conceal succession risk until the leader becomes ill, loses an election, or faces economic collapse.

**Conflict-sustaining resource flows**

Stakeholder capture → profitable scarcity or conflict → autonomous security actors → external sponsorship → weak domestic accountability → conflict becomes financially rational.

Reducing support to only one faction may not end conflict if other factions retain sponsors and revenue.

**Information-driven conflict**

Identity grievance → disinformation and threat narratives → hardened polarization → trust collapse → rejection of institutions → extra-institutional mobilization.

Information controls alone are insufficient if underlying grievances remain unresolved.

**Shock-induced collapse**

Weak resilience → visible crisis → unequal or illegitimate response → power consolidation → security mobilization → succession crisis and legitimacy collapse.

Technical crisis management without institutional trust, accountability, and protection of vulnerable groups can worsen the crisis.

**How to Code and Distinguish**

**GAF-5: Procedural Drag**

- **Scope:** Process-level dimension
- **Concerns:** Excessive approval steps, duplicated documentation, unclear sequencing, interagency referrals, unpredictable processing times
- **Remedies:** Process redesign, remove unnecessary steps, clarify authority, digitize, introduce time limits, create one-stop services
- **Key property:** Can occur inside an otherwise appropriately sized administration

**GAF-33: Administrative Overhang**

- **Scope:** System-level dimension
- **Concerns:** Overlapping agencies, mandates without funding, responsibilities without qualified personnel, excessive management layers, unsustainable payroll/pension obligations, territorial responsibilities beyond state reach
- **Remedies:** Merge or eliminate agencies, clarify mandates, devolve or discontinue responsibilities, align staffing with workload, prioritize core functions, match responsibilities to revenue and capacity
- **Key property:** Requires structural/organizational reform, not merely process redesign

**Suggested Coding Rule:**

|**Scenario**|**Code**|
|---|---|
|Primary problem is how a defined procedure operates|**GAF-5**|
|Primary problem is the size, structure, mandate, staffing, or fiscal burden of the administrative system|**GAF-33**|
|A structural mismatch directly produces process-level delay|**Both**|

**Diagnostic Test:** If the procedure were redesigned tomorrow, would the underlying institutional mismatch remain? If **yes** → GAF-33. If **no** → GAF-5.

**Part E: Diagnostic Procedure**

**Step 1: Define the crisis**

Identify:

- The failing outcome.
- Its magnitude and duration.
- Whether it is worsening.
- Who is affected.
- Which regions or groups are disproportionately affected.
- Whether the failure is visible to voters, media, businesses, and institutions.
- Whether it is framed as a governance problem or external inevitability.

**Step 2: Identify candidate GAF bottlenecks**

Begin with the factors most directly connected to the observed failure, then expand to linked factors.

For each candidate factor, record:

1.       Current state: healthy, bottleneck, uncertain, or not applicable.

2.       Evidence.

3.       Mechanism linking the factor to the observed outcome.

4.       Whether the factor is transient or structural.

5.       What evidence would falsify the diagnosis.

Do not classify all 32 factors automatically. Prioritize the few that plausibly explain the failure.

**Step 3a: Test interdependencies**

Ask:

- Which bottlenecks reinforce each other?
- Would fixing one factor leave the mechanism of failure intact?
- Does a proposed reform create a new veto point, legitimacy problem, equity problem, or reversal risk?
- Are there feedback loops?
- Which factor appears upstream?
- Which factors are symptoms rather than causes?

**Step 3b: Locate Hidden PCIL Factors**

**When PCIL-1-8 seem insufficient to explain persistence, check:**

- **Is institutional memory asymmetric? → PCIL-9**
- **Do different groups interpret institutional rules differently? → PCIL-10**
- **Are inherited categories treated as unchallengeable? → PCIL-11**
- **Does the institution deny historical harm while claiming neutrality? → PCIL-12**
- **Do minorities and majorities have opposite relationships with the same institution? → PCIL-13**
- **Is exclusion now automated/algorithmic, making it appear non-political? → PCIL-14**
- **Does one side control the documentary record? → PCIL-15**

**Step 4: Check PCIL root causes**

If relevant, map the bottleneck to PCIL-1 through PCIL-8.

Determine:

- Whether the PCIL factor plausibly predates the current crisis.
- Whether the bottleneck persisted through multiple governments or reform attempts.
- Whether the problem is structural or merely a current policy choice.
- Whether reform requires Stage 0 consensus.

**Stage 0 consensus**

Classify as:

- **YES:** PCIL-1, PCIL-2, PCIL-4, PCIL-6, PCIL-7, or PCIL-8 is central to the bottleneck.
- **NO:** The problem is mainly PCIL-3 regulatory-margin corruption or PCIL-5 procedural complexity without execution capacity.
- **CONDITIONAL:** Evidence is mixed, or the structural root cause is not yet established.

Stage 0 consensus means elite and institutional agreement that the current arrangement is unsustainable and must change. It is more likely when failure is highly visible, worsening, and recognized as a domestic governance problem.

**Step 5: Evaluate reform sequencing**

Use the following logic:

- **Single bottleneck:** A direct fix may work.
- **Two linked bottlenecks:** Fix both or the remaining one will recreate the failure.
- **Structural bottleneck:** Address the underlying institutional bargain before relying on technical reforms.
- **GAF-3 present:** Protect budgets and time horizons together.
- **GAF-15 high and worsening:** Identify whether a reform window exists.
- **GAF-15 low:** Investigate whether remittances, aid, optimistic metrics, or external explanations are concealing deterioration.
- **GAF-20 weak:** Design reforms to survive electoral and coalition changes.
- **GAF-24 present:** Explicitly compare alternative institutional models rather than optimizing only the existing one.

**Step 6: Research and verification**

When further research is needed, search for:

- Outcome and queue data.
- Staffing, vacancy, migration, and retention data.
- Budgets, funding formulas, and execution records.
- Approval timelines and project delays.
- Regulatory and court decisions.
- Institutional appointment and removal rules.
- Regional and distributional outcomes.
- Independent audits and post-mortems.
- Evidence of rents, capture, patronage, or conflicts of interest.
- External funding, military support, aid, remittances, sanctions, and resource flows.
- Local-language, community, academic, civil-society, and official sources.
- Evidence supporting competing explanations.

Use multiple independent sources. Do not treat search results, official claims, allegations, or AI-generated summaries as automatically reliable.

**How Administrative Overhang Cascades**

$\text{Administrative Overhang} \rightarrow \text{Staff & Budget Dilution} \rightarrow \text{Procedural Delay} \rightarrow \text{Discretion} \rightarrow \text{Patronage & Informal Payments} \rightarrow \text{Lower Trust & Weaker Implementation}$

This pathway is particularly relevant in post-colonial contexts where:

1.       **Institutional Orphaning** left inherited structures unsupervised

2.       **Patronage Institutionalization** filled capacity gaps with informal networks

3.       **Brain Drain** reduced the skilled professionals available to execute mandates

4.       **Regulatory Complexity** accumulated without being streamlined

The result: **A formally elaborate administrative system becomes functionally dependent on patronage because formal capacity cannot meet mandated obligations.**

**4. New: Invisible Persistence Mechanisms (Why Failures Survive Diagnosis)**

Seven mechanisms keep failures "invisible" even when factors are present:

1.       **Category Naturalization (PCIL-11)**: Inherited categories (ethnic, geographic, administrative) appear neutral/technical. Reclassification is treated as "administrative burden," not political necessity.

2.       **Institutional Amnesia (PCIL-9)**: Majority forgets why institution exists; minorities retain grievance. Asymmetry makes the memory gap itself invisible.

3.       **Presumption Reversal (PCIL-10)**: Majority claims accepted with minimal verification; minority claims require exhaustive proof. Asymmetry is procedurally neutral but substantively skewed.

4.       **Strategic Denial (PCIL-12)**: When past harm is documented, institutions respond with procedural correctness rather than accountability. Defense becomes indistinguishable from indifference.

5.       **Algorithmic Embedding (PCIL-14)**: Historical hierarchies reproduced at scale by code; bias appears technical, not political. Correction attempts fix outcomes without addressing origins.

6.       **Documentary Asymmetry (PCIL-15)**: One side has institutional records; the other has official silence reframed as "no evidence." Interpretation authority remains unequally distributed.

7.       **Intergenerational Transmission (PCIL-13)**: Majority learns institutions are "for them"; minorities learn institutions are adversarial. Differential institutional approach appears as "cultural attitude" rather than institutional behavior producing attitudes.
